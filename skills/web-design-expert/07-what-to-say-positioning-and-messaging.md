# 07 What to say: positioning and messaging

A site can look right and still say what every competitor says. That is a generic site. This file is about the words: what to decide before any design, how to check you are not saying the same as everyone else, and how to write a headline and a section. Where the evidence is a study, the file says who and how many. Where it is one expert's view, it says that. A lot of this field is opinion, and the file marks which parts.

Source format: (publisher, title, date, URL). Sources are saved in sources/messaging/.

## 1. Write the page before you design it

Rule of thumb: write the headline, the sections and the proof in plain text first, and only then draw the layout.

Why: layout is a container for words. If you design first, the words get squeezed to fit boxes that were drawn for words nobody had written yet, and you end up with a headline that is a size and a shape before it is a message.

Evidence: this is practitioner opinion, not a study. A government digital team describes working content first with a content designer and an interaction designer, to speed up prototypes and cut rounds of change. It also warns that a wireframe can give the illusion of a finished product (Defra Digital blog, "Doing content first design at Defra part one", 17 Mar 2026, https://defradigital.blog.gov.uk/2026/03/17/doing-content-first-design-at-defra-part-one, seen through a search summary only). Vendor blogs that say the same are marketing for their own services, so do not lean on them. One fair caution from that search: content-first does not mean every word is final before a line is drawn. It means the message and the order of the page are settled first.

What Claude should do: before showing a wireframe, ask for or draft the one-line offer, the three or four things a visitor needs to know, and the proof. Put real words in the wireframe boxes, never "Heading here".

On a phone: write for the narrow screen first. If the headline needs more than about two lines at phone width, it is probably too long (this is our rule of thumb, not a sourced number).

Common mistake: lorem ipsum. A layout approved with fake words says nothing about whether the real words fit or work.

## 2. Positioning for a website: April Dunford's five parts

Rule of thumb: do the positioning thinking before the homepage, because the homepage is where it shows.

April Dunford is a positioning consultant and author of Obviously Awesome. Her method is published free on her blog, and it is one expert's framework, not a tested law. It has been widely used; no controlled study of it was found. She lists five parts and says to work through them in order. The five parts below are paraphrased, not her exact names (April Dunford, "A Quickstart Guide to Positioning", 15 Mar 2021, https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning):

1. Competitive alternatives. What would a customer do if you did not exist? Often the answer is nothing, or a spreadsheet, or the way they do it now.
2. Unique attributes. What do you have that those alternatives do not?
3. Value. The "so what" for the customer of each attribute.
4. Target customers. The people who care a lot about that value.
5. Market category. The kind of thing you are, framed so that your value is obvious to those people.

Two points from her that matter for a small site. First, the real competitor is usually the status quo, not the other firm in your category. She writes, "In enterprise software, we typically lose between 20% and 30% of deals to 'no decision'", and links an industry article that cites CSO Insights at 21.3%. That is an industry figure, not her own record and not her own study (Dunford, "Positioning and Competition", 28 Dec 2020, https://www.aprildunford.com/post/positioning-and-competition). Second, she warns against "phantom competitors", firms your customers never actually weigh you against. A homepage written against the wrong rivals dilutes the message.

Her post on storytelling says positioning comes before the story (Dunford, "Storytelling vs Positioning", 27 Apr 2020, https://www.aprildunford.com/post/storytelling-vs-positioning).

Her guidance on the homepage itself: no post of hers that gives it was found. Second-hand summaries of her podcast ("Positioning with April Dunford", episode of 2 Apr 2026) say she suggests writing a messaging document before touching the homepage, and that she distrusts homepage A/B tests as a way of testing positioning, preferring real sales conversations. Treat that as unconfirmed, since it comes from search summaries read on 18 Oct 2026. The five parts translate to a page like this, and this mapping is ours, not hers: the headline names the category and the value for the target customer, the first section shows what the alternatives cannot do, and the proof backs the unique attributes.

What Claude should do: ask the five questions in order before writing a headline. If the person cannot answer "what do your customers do today instead of hiring you", say the page will be vague until they can.

On a phone: the positioning has to survive on one small screen. If the target customer and the value do not both fit in the first screen of text, the positioning is not clear enough yet.

Common mistake: starting from the list of features, which Dunford says "just took too long" and gave positioning that "sounded good in the office, but it didn't work with customers".

## 3. The sea of sameness, and how to check for it

Rule of thumb: if a competitor could put their logo on your headline and it would still be true, rewrite it.

The check, in four steps. Open five competitor homepages. For each, write down the headline, the sub-line and the first three claims. Count the words that appear on three or more of them (a list of usual words, which is ours and has no source: "solutions", "innovative", "trusted", "tailored", "results-driven", "world-class"). Then read your own draft against that list.

There are two tests with an actual source. Jakob Nielsen's competitor-tagline test: collect your tagline and the taglines of your three strongest competitors, take the names off, and see whether anyone can tell which company does what. Ask people outside your business too. His second test is the opposite test: rewrite your main copy to say the reverse. If no company would ever say the reverse (nobody advertises "buggy, fragmented, inefficient, and expensive"), then your copy tells the visitor nothing (Nielsen Norman Group, "Tagline Blues: What's the Site About?", 21 Jul 2001, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tagline-blues-whats-the-site-about/). Grade: one expert's column from 2001. It reports no participants. It is a sound test because it needs none, not because a study proved it.

How common is the problem? In NN/g's reviews of homepages, 36% followed the guideline "Include a tag line that explicitly summarizes what the site or company does", and 27% followed "Emphasize what your site offers that's of value to users and how your services differ from those of key competitors" (NN/g, "The Ten Most Violated Homepage Design Guidelines", 9 Nov 2003, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/most-violated-homepage-guidelines/). Grade: these are old, and Nielsen says the sample skews to large organisations that had paid for a review. Do not treat the percentages as today's rate. Treat them as evidence that this fault is old and common.

NN/g on a related point: avoid taglines that could describe thousands of other companies, and replace marketing jargon with a few concrete facts (NN/g, "Great Summaries on 'About Us' Pages Engage Users and Build Trust", 21 Jun 2015, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/about-us-summaries/). The 2015 article refers to several studies but gives no sample sizes.

On a phone: the sameness shows up fastest here, because the headline and a button are all there is on screen. Run the logo test on the phone view.

Common mistake: checking competitors only on design. The test is on words.

## 4. Distinctiveness versus differentiation: what the Ehrenberg-Bass people argue

This matters because the usual advice, "be different", has been challenged by researchers, and Claude should know where the argument stands before telling a client to "differentiate".

What they argue. Jenni Romaniuk, Byron Sharp and Andrew Ehrenberg (2007) report that perceived differentiation between competing brands is low across many categories in two countries, yet people keep buying them, and conclude that differentiation plays a smaller part in brand strategy than the textbooks assume (Australasian Marketing Journal 15(2), 2007, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1016/S1441-3582(07)70042-3; the journal page was blocked, so the abstract was read through a search summary of a copy). A replication by Millward Brown (Nigel Hollis), using its BrandZ database of over 6,000 brands over 10 years, found that among people who consider a brand acceptable an average of 18% called it "different from others" (the survey item was "different from other brands"), with a range of 0 to 86%. It is reported on Byron Sharp's blog, which calls the replicators "an independent group of researchers" (Byron Sharp's blog, 26 Jun 2011, https://byronsharp.com/blog/files/millwardbrown_confirmation.html). Grade: survey evidence of what people say.

What they recommend instead is distinctiveness: being easy to recognise and easy to remember through consistent names, colours, logos and other cues. Romaniuk's work asks whether a cue is both famous (buyers link it to the brand) and unique (they link it only to that brand).

The critique. A trade-press piece argues that the two ideas overlap, because a distinctive feature that buyers value is both. It says asking people whether a brand is "different" is an odd question, stated views may not match buying, and "unique" is defined very narrowly. The author thinks the data support a narrower claim: unique associations do not predict preference or penetration, which is not the same as saying differentiation is unimportant (Rob Meyerson, BrandKnew, "What does Byron Sharp's research really tell us about differentiation?", 31 Jul 2021, https://www.brandknewmag.com/?p=21380). He retells a study by Romaniuk and Gaillard of 94 brands in eight categories with 43 attributes, in which unique associations were no more valuable in choice than shared ones. Grade: an opinion piece, but it states the study details.

Our reading for a small business site, which is opinion: both sides agree you must be easy to recognise and remember, and it costs nothing to be so. Where they differ is whether to chase a claim nobody else makes. For a marketer the safe working position is this. Say plainly what you do and for whom (so you are found and understood), repeat the same name, colour and phrase everywhere (distinctiveness), and add a differentiating claim only when it is true, specific and something buyers care about. Do not invent a difference to fill a gap.

On a phone: distinctiveness is cheap on a small screen. A consistent logo, one accent colour and one repeated phrase are visible straight away.

Common mistake: treating "distinctive" as "looks odd". It means easily recognised as you.

## 5. How people read on the web

Rule of thumb: write for scanning. Short, plain, and the point first.

The study everyone cites. In 1997, Nielsen Norman Group built five versions of a made-up tourism site with the same information and different writing: promotional (the control), concise, scannable, objective, and all three combined. The measurement study had 51 experienced web users, and the work as a whole covered 81 users in three studies. Overall usability, with the control as 100, came out at 158 for concise, 147 for scannable, 127 for objective and 224 for the combined version, which NN/g reports as a 124% improvement. The score is the geometric mean of five measures: task time, errors, memory, time using the sitemap and satisfaction (NN/g, "Concise, SCANNABLE, and Objective: How to Write for the Web", 1997, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/).

Limits, said plainly. It is 1997, the site was invented for the test, the users were experienced web users, and no published sample for each condition was found. The direction (plainer and shorter helped) has been repeated in later NN/g writing, but the exact percentages are specific to that experiment. Do not say "plain copy improves usability by 124%" as a general fact.

The same year's companion piece says 79% of test users always scanned new pages and 16% read word by word. That page gives no sample size (NN/g, "How Users Read on the Web", 30 Sep 1997, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/). Its advice: meaningful subheadings (not clever ones), one idea per paragraph, the conclusion first, and about half the words of conventional writing.

Marketese, in NN/g's wording, is boastful promotional language. In that study the objective version beat the promotional one by 27% on the overall score, and the study says promotional claims "may distract users from processing the meaning". Related: NN/g on About pages says vague marketing language such as "revolutionary" can confuse people or make them distrust the site (NN/g, 21 Jun 2015, link above).

On a phone: scanning is stronger on a small screen, and a long paragraph looks like a wall. Two or three short lines per paragraph is a fair target (our rule, not sourced).

Common mistake: long intro paragraphs that restate the company's mission before saying what it does.

## 6. Headlines that say what you do

Rule of thumb: the headline should answer "what is this and is it for me" in plain words, with the key word at the front.

NN/g's homepage guidance: treat the homepage like an elevator pitch that says what the organisation does and what visitors can do there; add a short tagline unless the name already says it; swap generic welcome messages for a line that says what the site is for; avoid jargon and feature names that need explaining (NN/g, "Homepage Design: 5 Fundamental Principles", 15 Mar 2024, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/). Nielsen's 2002 version is one sentence: start with a tagline that summarises what the site or company does, which matters most for lesser-known firms (NN/g, "Top 10 Guidelines for Homepage Usability", 11 May 2002, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-ten-guidelines-for-homepage-usability/). Grade: expert guidelines from NN/g's long practice. They are widely followed, not proved by one controlled test.

On headline craft, NN/g gives five tips: make it work out of context, offer something specific and useful, avoid cute or trendy language, cut non-essential words, and put the key words first because readers focus on the first few words. Its example pair: broad, "Make better, faster decisions with data visualization"; concrete, "Data visualization helps you detect and prevent fraud faster" (NN/g, "Headings Are Pick-Up Lines: 5 Tips for Writing Headlines That Convert", 9 Aug 2015, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/headings-pickup-lines/). The article does not report its own study, so grade it as guidance.

Template that works for most small firms (our suggestion, opinion): "[What you do] for [who], so they can [outcome]." Then check it with the logo test in section 3.

On a phone: two lines at most, and the first three words should carry the meaning, because a small screen may cut the line.

Common mistake: a clever headline that needs the picture to make sense. Another: a welcome message.

## 7. Specifics over adjectives

Rule of thumb: every claim that could be a number, a name or a place should be one.

NN/g says exact figures read as more credible than "thousands", and that numerals attract the eye more than spelled-out numbers (NN/g, "Show Numbers as Numerals When Writing for Online Readers", 15 Apr 2007, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/web-writing-show-numbers-as-numerals/). Grade: the eyetracking observation comes from NN/g's studies with no participant numbers given. The credibility claim is asserted, not measured. Nielsen's 2002 homepage guidelines (link in section 6) make a related point about showing real content, not about numbers over adjectives.

Nielsen's point about adjectives (2001, link in section 3): words like robust, integrated and efficient say nothing, because no firm would claim the opposite.

Worked example of the shift (both examples are made up for illustration). "Trusted by leading brands" becomes "Used by 14 marketing teams, nine of them in financial services." "Fast, reliable delivery" becomes "Posted by 2pm, with you the next day." Only write what is true and that the client can show.

On a phone: a number in large type is one of the few things that survives a quick thumb scroll.

Common mistake: inventing a number. Never. If there is no number, say what is true without one.

## 8. Proof: testimonials, case studies, client lists

Rule of thumb: put proof next to the claim it supports, and make it checkable.

What the evidence says. It is thinner than the web's confidence suggests. No controlled study was found in the sources read that measures which kind of testimonial is most credible. Vendor blogs quote conversion lifts for badges and testimonials, but none found cited a primary study, so they are not used.

What can be grounded: in NN/g's About Us research (three rounds, over 70 users, 100 sites tested and 65 reviewed; latest round 20 users), people read testimonials on company sites but cross-checked reviews on third-party sites such as Google Reviews, and they rated reviews and recommendations from trusted peers among the most important factors in forming an impression. NN/g advises honest, balanced testimonials (NN/g, "'About Us' Information on Corporate Websites - User Research Findings", 26 May 2019, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/about-us-information-on-websites/). Stanford's web credibility guidelines, built on what it says is three years of research with over 4,500 people (some of it preliminary or unpublished), say to make information easy to verify, show a real organisation with an address, show real people with their credentials, and make contact easy (Stanford Persuasive Technology Lab, "Stanford Guidelines for Web Credibility", 2002, https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines).

Practical reading, which is our inference: a named person with a role and a company, a specific result, and a way to check it (a link, a case study) is more believable than an unsigned "Great service!". NN/g also found stock photography counted against sites; use real photos of real work and people.

On a phone: a single testimonial in a card, with name and photo, reads better than a carousel. Carousels hide proof behind a swipe (our view; page 05 covers it).

Common mistake: a client logo strip with no story behind it. A logo shows you were hired. It does not say what you did. Only show logos you have permission to show.

## 9. Tone

Rule of thumb: sound like a competent person talking to one reader.

Stanford's guideline 9 is "Use restraint with any promotional content (e.g., ads, offers)", which is about ads. Its separate writing line asks for text that is "clear, direct, and sincere" (credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines, link above). NN/g's 1997 piece says promotional claims may distract users from processing the meaning, and its 2015 piece says vague marketing language can make people distrust a site. Beyond that, tone choice (formal, friendly, funny) is opinion. Pick it from the reader: a finance director and a festival organiser want different registers. Keep one voice across the site. No evidence is claimed for tone.

Common mistake: a voice that changes from page to page because three people wrote it.

## 10. Write for the reader's problem, and the About page

Rule of thumb: lead with the visitor's situation and what changes for them. Put your history after.

Why: Stanford lists "be easy to use and useful" and warns against showing off technology or company ego. NN/g's 2015 article found people peek at top-level About pages for quick answers and go deeper only if the firm seems worth it; its 2019 study found some skip About altogether and search elsewhere (NN/g, 21 Jun 2015 and 26 May 2019, links above).

About page, from NN/g's work: say what you do in clear, jargon-free words; give a brief summary with a few concrete, impressive facts; show real photos of staff; offer more than one way to make contact; keep the story the same across channels; and link About clearly from the footer. NN/g's 2019 study had 20 users in its latest round and produced 85 guidelines, so this is among the better-grounded areas in this file. Its negatives list included stock photography, walls of text, and hard-to-find "what you do" and contact information.

Suggested order (opinion): one sentence on what you do and for whom, two or three facts, who the people are (photos, roles), how you work, how to get in touch. Founding story last and short.

On a phone: About pages are often read on phones after a search. Keep the summary at the top and the contact details within reach.

Common mistake: starting with "Founded in 1998 by...". The reader does not yet care.

## 11. Short checklist

For a headline:
1. Does it say what you do and for whom in plain words?
2. Would it still be true with a competitor's logo on it? If so, rewrite.
3. Would anyone ever say the opposite? If not, it says nothing.
4. Is the key word in the first three words?
5. Does it fit in two lines at phone width?
6. Is there a number, name or place that could replace an adjective?

For a section:
1. One idea. One job. Can you say it in a sentence?
2. Does it open with the point, then the support?
3. Is there proof within one screen of the claim?
4. Is it written about the reader, not about us?
5. Is there a single next step?
6. Does it still make sense read on a phone with only the headings visible?

## What is evidence and what is opinion

Evidence with a stated method: NN/g 1997 writing experiment (51 to 81 users, old, invented site); NN/g About Us research (over 70 users across rounds, 2019); Romaniuk, Sharp and Ehrenberg 2007 and the BrandZ replication (Millward Brown survey data, reported on Sharp's blog); Stanford credibility work (4,500 people claimed, partly preliminary).

Expert opinion or guidelines: Dunford's five parts; Nielsen's taglines tests; NN/g homepage and headline tips; content-first working.

Weak or missing: what makes a testimonial credible; whether a clearer homepage headline lifts sales; Dunford's own homepage guidance.
